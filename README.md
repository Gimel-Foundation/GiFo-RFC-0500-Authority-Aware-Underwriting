# GiFo-RFC-0500 (V0.9)

Authority Aware Underwriting - How GAuth Supports Liability Analysis and Loss Control

New Request for Comments of Gimel Foundation (GiFo RFC) - Authority Aware Underwriting

Abstract of RFC: GAuth enforces delegated authority. Insurance transfers specified financial
consequences of risk. An authorised action can still be erroneous; an accurate action
can still be unauthorised. These distinctions matter both before a loss and when
allocating responsibility afterwards.
For an underwriter, the proposition is straightforward: an agent’s identity and access
rights do not necessarily establish the financial or operational authority given to it for a
particular assignment. GAuth adds a separately evidenced instruction from the insured
or other responsible principal: what this agent may do, for whom, for how long and
within which limits. When that instruction is checked before execution, it can become a
testable loss-prevention control rather than a statement of intent.
This RFC connects that control to the insurance lifecycle: submission, risk engineering,
quotation and binding, ongoing exposure monitoring, claims investigation and renewal.
It does not make the insurer an approver of individual transactions. Section 3.1
describes the normal course of business; §4.4 compares the same loss scenario with
and without GAuth; §8.4 explains the incremental underwriting value over existing
safeguards.

Gimel proposes collaboration with insurers around three contributions:

1. Authority evidence for liability analysis: establish what authority existed, what
the agent did, what the relying party received or verified, and how those facts
relate to a loss.

2. Loss control: restrict the executable consequences of model errors, prompt-
induced instructions and unauthorised delegation through enforced authority
boundaries.

3. Evidence-based underwriting: test whether demonstrated controls reduce loss
frequency, severity or aggregate exposure su\iciently to influence coverage terms
or pricing.

An agent exceeding its authority while a relying party knowingly accepts the action
presents a di\erent factual situation from an agent exceeding authority without that
party receiving the limitation. GAuth-related evidence can help distinguish these situations. 
It does not itself determine legal knowledge, causation, liability or insurance
coverage.

As an example, Munich Re’s aiSure and Ergo Next illustrate AI-performance risk
assessment and financial risk transfer. Instnt illustrates identity/fraud-risk decisioning
combined with insurance. Neither is described here as a substitute for action-specific
delegated authority. All examples illustrate potential complements; the proposed
approach remains open to other insurers and technology providers.
